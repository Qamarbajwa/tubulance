\subsection*{3. Why One Dimension Cannot Twist: Lines vs.\ Locked Planes}

Before we talk about how our measuring machine works, we need to understand a simple geometric truth about dimensions.

Take a single piece of string---a \textbf{one-dimensional line}. You can slide beads back and forth along that string, or wrap the string around a cylinder like a wedding ring. That is what mathematicians call an \textbf{Abelian ($U(1)$)} system. Every motion along a single line commutes: sliding two inches forward and three inches back is the exact same thing as sliding three inches back and two inches forward. Because there is no sideways direction to collide with, a 1D line cannot tie itself into a knot.

Now lay a second string across the first one so they form a \textbf{two-dimensional plane}, and add a third so you have a \textbf{three-dimensional corner}. Try rotating a book by 90 degrees around the X-axis and then 90 degrees around the Y-axis. Now do it in reverse: Y-axis first, then X-axis. The book ends up facing a completely different direction! That mismatch---the failure of rotations in different planes to commute---is \textbf{non-Abelian curvature}.

Across all four of our projects, reality flips a switch at the exact boundary where \textbf{1D lines cross into 2D planes}:

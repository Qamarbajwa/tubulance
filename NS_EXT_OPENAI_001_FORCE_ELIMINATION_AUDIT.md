## 1. The Core Analytical Dilemma: $R = \mathcal{O}(\tau^\infty) \implies R \equiv 0$?

The OpenAI construction constructs a solution pair $(u, p)$ such that the Navier–Stokes momentum residual $R(u, p)$ satisfies:
$$\lim_{\tau \to 0^+} \tau^{-N} \|R(u, p)\|_{L^\infty} = 0 \quad \forall N \in \mathbb{N}.$$

To achieve Clay Millennium Prize Statement B on $\mathbb{T}^3$, we must achieve:
$$R(u, p) \equiv 0 \quad \text{identically on } \mathbb{T}^3 \times [0, 1).$$

We audit five candidate mathematical mechanisms for eliminating this residual.

---

## 2. Audit of Candidate Elimination Mechanisms

### 2.1 Mechanism 1: Infinite Convergent Asymptotic Series
- **Proposal**: Instead of stopping at a finite order $N$ and assigning the remainder to $f$, sum the infinite series of corrections:
  $$u = u^{(0)} + \sum_{n=0}^\infty \left( w^{(n)} + \bar{u}^{(n)} \right).$$
- **Audit**:
  In asymptotic analysis (Borel's lemma), every formal power series $\sum a_n \tau^n$ corresponds to a smooth function, but the series itself is almost generically **divergent** (Gevrey class 1 or 2).
  In the Navier–Stokes equations, the number of nonlinear cross-interaction terms at order $n$ grows factorially:
  $$\|u^{(n+1)}\| \sim n! \, C^n q^n.$$
  This factorial divergence is the hallmark of hydrodynamic asymptotic expansions. Summing the series introduces an infinite-order Borel sum whose remainder is non-zero and flat ($\sim \exp(-c/q)$).
- **Verdict**: **FALSIFIED**. Factorial divergence prevents naive infinite summation.

---

### 2.2 Mechanism 2: Nash–Moser Inverse Function Theorem
- **Proposal**: Use a hard implicit function theorem (Nash–Moser) with smoothing operators $S_\theta$ to invert the linearized Navier–Stokes operator $\mathcal{L}_u v = -R$ around the approximate blowup solution $u$.
- **Audit**:
  Nash–Moser requires:
  1. Tame estimates for the linearized inverse $\mathcal{L}_u^{-1}$:
     $$\|\mathcal{L}_u^{-1} g\|_{H^s} \le C \left( \|g\|_{H^{s+d}} + \|u\|_{H^{s+d}} \|g\|_{L^2} \right).$$
  2. The background state $u$ must remain in a bounded Sobolev space or have controlled loss of derivatives.
  However, in the singular core:
  $$\|u(\cdot, t)\|_{H^s} \sim \tau^{-1/2 - h - s/2} \xrightarrow[\tau \to 0^+]{} \infty.$$
  The loss of derivatives and singular growth of the background profile $u$ as $\tau \to 0$ cause the loss parameter $d$ to blow up. The linearized operator $\mathcal{L}_u$ develops a continuous spectrum crossing into the unstable half-plane (hyperbolic instability), violating the tame invertibility hypotheses of Nash–Moser.
- **Verdict**: **FALSIFIED**. Hyper-singular loss of derivatives blocks Nash–Moser inversion.

---

### 2.3 Mechanism 3: Fixed-Point Contraction on the Flat Remainder
- **Proposal**: Let $u = u_{\text{approx}} + v$, where $v$ is a small perturbation solving:
  $$\partial_t v + (u_{\text{approx}} \cdot \nabla) v + (v \cdot \nabla) u_{\text{approx}} + (v \cdot \nabla) v - \nu \Delta v + \nabla q = - R(u_{\text{approx}}).$$
  Since $R \sim \exp(-1/\tau)$ is exponentially small, can Picard iteration or Banach fixed-point contract?
- **Audit**:
  To contract, the linear propagator $U(t, s)$ generated by $\partial_t + (u_{\text{approx}} \cdot \nabla) + (\cdot \cdot \nabla) u_{\text{approx}} - \nu \Delta$ must have bounded growth.
  The stretching rate of $u_{\text{approx}}$ is:
  $$\lambda_{\max}(S) \asymp \tau^{-1 - h}.$$
  The growth of linearized perturbations is governed by the Grönwall exponent:
  $$\|U(t, 0)\| \sim \exp\left( \int_0^t \tau^{-1 - h} dt' \right) = \exp\left( \frac{1}{h \tau^h} \right).$$
  Comparing the flat source to the growth of perturbations:
  $$\text{Source size: } \|R\| \sim \exp\left(-\frac{1}{\tau}\right).$$
  $$\text{Linear amplification: } \|U\| \sim \exp\left(+\frac{1}{h \tau^h}\right).$$
  Notice the exponents!
  For small $h \in (0, 1)$, $\frac{1}{\tau} \gg \frac{1}{h \tau^h}$ as $\tau \to 0$.
  At first glance, $\exp(-1/\tau)$ decays faster than $\exp(1/\tau^h)$ grows!
  **However**: The problem is the spatial gradient loss. The term $(v \cdot \nabla) u_{\text{approx}}$ requires controlling $\nabla v$. Estimating $\nabla v$ introduces a loss of derivatives that forces the norm to jump to higher Sobolev spaces $H^{s+1}$, where the source norm $\|R\|_{H^s}$ carries factorials $(k!)^2$ that overwhelm the exponential damping.
- **Verdict**: **UNRESOLVED / CURRENTLY UNSUPPORTED**. While an alluring avenue, no functional framework currently exists that closes this fixed-point argument without external regularization.

---

### 2.4 Mechanism 4: Force Internalization via Additional Wave Families
- **Proposal**: Instead of assigning $f = R$, represent $f$ as the divergence of a new, secondary set of high-frequency oscillatory waves $w^{\text{sec}}$:
  $$f = - \operatorname{div} \langle w^{\text{sec}} \otimes w^{\text{sec}} \rangle + \dots$$
- **Audit**:
  To internalize $f$, the stress cone must have enough degrees of freedom to span the full vector field $f \in \mathbb{R}^3$.
  In 3D, the Reynolds stress tensor $\langle w \otimes w \rangle$ is a $3 \times 3$ symmetric matrix with 6 independent components.
  - The vector field $f$ has 3 components. By the Helmholtz–Hodge decomposition, incompressibility eliminates the gradient part (absorbed into pressure $\nabla p$), leaving 2 solenoidal degrees of freedom.
  - While kinematically possible, dynamically this requires a **third and fourth wave family** $v_3, v_4 \in \mathbb{R}^3$ that are themselves sustained by the flow.
  - But these secondary waves $w^{\text{sec}}$ must also be seeded! This merely pushes the seeding problem ($F1$) back one level, requiring a tertiary force to seed the secondary waves.
- **Verdict**: **FALSIFIED (Circular Regress)**. Internalizing $F2$ via waves re-triggers the $F1$ seeding barrier.
